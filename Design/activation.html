Why this is the first question

A blank page is the single most common reason a non-technical person abandons setup. Six choices load a real starting point, and the panel below shows you exactly what arrived so it never feels like something was decided behind your back.

Six options, not three

The famous “too much choice hurts” finding does not replicate reliably — the real risk is options that are hard to tell apart. These six are distinguishable in two seconds, which is the test that actually matters.

The most valuable thing Subiza does

It answers at nine on a Sunday evening. That's why hours come this early — they draw the boundary that frames the whole product, and they're what later produces the number that keeps people subscribed: calls you would have missed.

What we don't tell you

We never say “this takes ten minutes.” Stating a duration up front measurably reduces completion on anything over five minutes. The target exists — it's ten minutes to the test call — but it's ours to hit, not yours to watch.

A price is a field, not a paragraph

No other product in this category treats a price list as a structured table. Everyone else flattens it into a PDF or a text blob, and that is precisely where invented numbers come from. Here a price is a typed row: read out loud, never generated.

Checked before anything is sent

Sharpness, glare and framing are checked on the device before a single byte is uploaded. Data can cost a real share of a month's income here, and spending it on a photo we already know is unreadable would be a small theft.

It will not be perfect

OCR on a photographed printed list tops out around 85%, and handwriting is far worse. That's why the confirmation table is compulsory rather than optional — and why we flag the three rows we're unsure about instead of asking you to re-read all eight.

It says your name

Every preview says your actual greeting with your actual business name. Hearing “Muraho, ni Salon Ubwiza” in a natural voice costs us nothing and does real work — it is the first moment the product stops being an idea.

Nothing plays on its own

Audio only plays when you tap. That's WCAG 1.4.2, and it's also simply how browsers now work — an autoplaying preview is silently blocked on most first visits, which reads to the user as a broken product.

Your own voice isn't here

Cloning needs a separate consent from the person whose voice it is, a spoken verification, and a clean minute of audio. Putting that in the critical path would add minutes and a legal artefact to a flow that has to stay short.

This is the screen that matters

Activation is not signing up, and it is not finishing a checklist. It is hearing your own agent answer a question about your own business, in your own language, and judging the answer good.

Everything before this is setup. Everything after it is adoption.

Why a real call, not a browser demo

Because this is a telephone product. Hearing it over a real GSM line, with real narrowband audio and real latency, is the honest demonstration. A browser demo would sound better and prove less.

Three routes, because this step is not allowed to fail

We call you. If that fails, you talk to it in the browser. If that fails, you dial a number yourself. Nobody leaves because of this screen.

This is where the product teaches its own mind

In ninety seconds you learn four things: it answers from what I told it, I can see why, I can fix it, and the fix works immediately. That understanding is what makes a person comfortable letting it talk to real customers — which is why the correction loop lives here, not in a settings screen nobody opens.

No confidence numbers during the call

While you're on the call, a confidence score would create anxiety with nothing you could do about it. Afterwards it's useful — it's what decides which turns get flagged for you here.

The one step that can't be skipped

Every other step in part three can be skipped. This one can't, because an agent with nowhere to send a customer who needs a person should not be answering the phone at all.

The one place we genuinely can't help

A forwarding code is a command from your SIM to your carrier. No website can send it. What we can do is call your number thirty seconds later and tell you it worked — and that verification is what turns an act of faith into a fact.

And it's skippable

Skip it and the agent stays fully usable in test mode, with Home carrying exactly one outstanding action. Nothing is locked behind it.

The reason people say yes

You are not being asked to hand your customers to a machine. You are being asked to let it pick up the calls that currently ring out — a decision with no downside, because the alternative is nobody answering at all.

You raise it yourself

We won't nag you to widen it. You do that yourself from the status pill on Home, when it has earned it — and you can narrow it again in one tap, which is the part that makes widening it safe to try.

The list starts part-done

You don't arrive at an empty list. You arrive at one that is already more than half ticked — and that difference is measurable, not decorative.

Flow 1.3 · atlas F-06

Guided activation

The flow the company lives or dies on. Nine steps, then a checklist. The design target is first value in under ten minutes and fully live in under forty-five — and that target is ours to hit, never a number shown to the owner.

01

The inversion

Every comparable product asks for the channel connection first, because it is technically the enabling step. Subiza asks for it fourth from last, because it is emotionally the hardest and it is riddled with external failure — Meta queues of 5–15 working days, carrier KYC, OTP delivery, GSM codes.

Everyone else

Connect → configure → hope

Asking a shop owner to dial a forwarding code before they have any evidence the thing works is asking for faith. Most of them decline, quietly, by never coming back.

Subiza

Configure → hear it work → connect

Asking after they have heard it quote their own prices in Kinyarwanda is asking for a small favour. Same request, different moment, completely different answer.

Activation is a moment, not a milestone. Not “signed up”, not “connected WhatsApp”, not “finished the checklist”. It is: a customer interaction was handled by the AI, and the owner watched it happen and judged the answer good. Every decision on this page is reverse-engineered from reaching that moment quickly.

Open question 6.a, carried forward. Is nine steps too many? The honest answer is that nobody knows yet — the evidence says clarity matters more than step count, but most published “multi-step form abandonment” statistics turn out to cite studies that do not exist. This needs testing with real Rwandan SME owners, not another blog post.

03

What the research changed

Removed

The “ten minutes” promise

A meta-analysis of 32 experiments found progress indicators hurt completion when an overall duration is stated for anything over five minutes. The target is real and it stays internal. The nine marks show position, never a countdown, and they sit under the header rather than looming over it.

Kept, and now justified

The part-done checklist

Nunes & Drèze's endowed-progress field experiment: a card needing eight stamps completed by 19%; the same eight stamps on a ten-stamp card with two already given completed by 34%. Home shows six of eleven already ticked — and every one of those six is genuinely done.

Reconsidered

Six business types, not three

The famous jam study is real but does not replicate — the field's own meta-analysis puts choice overload at roughly zero in general, with the effect appearing only when options are similar and hard to compare. So six clearly-different business types are fine; the voice picker is where the risk actually lives, so that one stays at four with labels you can tell apart without listening.

Added

An on-device quality check

Blur, glare and framing are checked on the handset before a byte is uploaded. Data costs up to a meaningful share of monthly income for the poorest users here; spending it on a photo already known to be unreadable is not a rounding error.

Added

The upload never blocks the wizard

Compressed on-device, uploaded in the background, resumable from the last chunk rather than restarted. The owner picks a voice while the photo is still going up, and gets told when it lands.

Corrected

No confidence scores during the call

Interim ASR text appears immediately in a lighter style and is replaced by the final text, never appended. A live confidence number would create anxiety with nothing the owner could do about it mid-call. After the call it's exactly what decides which turns get flagged.

04

The price table — the atlas's real innovation

No product in this category treats a price list as structured data. Everyone flattens it into a PDF or a text blob and lets a language model read it back, which is precisely where invented numbers come from. Here a price is a typed row: read out loud, never generated.

The test call cannot be allowed to fail

1 · We call their phoneFull production pipeline. Real GSM, real 8 kHz, real latency
2 · Not answered → talk to it in the browserNeeds no phone at all, and works if they've stepped away
3 · Still nothing → they dial our numberWorks from a feature phone, works with no data
tenant.activatedFires when a test call completes and the transcript has been viewed

Outbound, not a browser widget, and that is a real trade. A browser demo is easier to build and would sound better — wideband audio, no carrier in the path. It would also prove less, because the product's actual medium is a narrowband phone call. The atlas leaves this open (question 6.c); this design takes the honest demonstration and keeps the browser test as the first fallback rather than the default.

The live transcript is doing three jobs at once. It proves the AI understood — the owner watches their own Kinyarwanda appear as text. It introduces the Inbox before the Inbox exists. And it makes “why did it say that” feel like a natural question later, because they have already watched the words arrive.

06

The trust ladder

Three rungs, and the recommended one is the narrowest. This is the reason people say yes.

RungWhat it meansThe owner's calculation
Only when I'm closed · recommendedDaytime calls come to you untouched. Evenings, nights, Sundays it answersNo downside exists. Those calls currently ring out to nobody. Anything the agent does is strictly better than silence
Only when I don't answerYour phone rings 20 seconds first, every timeSmall downside, large gain. Usually chosen in week two
All callsIt answers first, alwaysA real decision with a real downside. Nobody should make it on day one

Staged autonomy is under-evidenced, and worth doing anyway. The published material on permission ladders is reasoning rather than measurement, and none of it studies non-technical users. The one hard number available is from a different context entirely: users approved ~93% of permission prompts anyway, which says blanket asking produces rubber-stamping rather than safety. The design conclusion holds — default to the safest scope, make widening it a thing the owner reaches for, and make narrowing it a single tap so that widening feels reversible.
