That's a lot of rules

Instruction-following degrades as rule counts rise — in one study, at 80 simultaneous rules no model got them all right, in any format. You're nowhere near that. But rules that overlap are better merged than added, and the ones you've switched off don't count against you at all.

Why we catch it and the model doesn't

A model can usually detect that two instructions conflict — and almost never says so. In one study GPT-4o answered without mentioning the contradiction 97.5% of the time, and the best model surfaced it in fewer than half of cases. There's also a recency bias: whichever rule you wrote last quietly wins. None of that is a decision you made.

We'll record that you chose to. If the behaviour turns out odd, this is the first thing we'll point at.

Every change can be undone

This is the whole reason editing feels safe. An owner who is afraid to change something doesn't change it, and their agent stays exactly as it was on day one — which is the slow way to lose them.

You can undo an undo

Restoring an old version saves the current one first, as its own version. So there is never a state you can't get back out of — which is the difference between a safety net and a trapdoor.

Changes apply to the next conversation, never to one already in progress

One decision, and it has a critic

Every product in this category offers a simple mode and a complex one — and makes you choose at creation, permanently. A business that starts simple and grows has to rebuild. Here it's one agent with two views, and switching costs nothing.

But Nielsen says don't

Writing in 2026 about AI agents specifically, Jakob Nielsen rejected Simple/Advanced toggles outright: they “fracture codebases and documentation” and assume power users who mostly don't exist — most people are perpetual intermediates. That's a real objection and it deserves a real answer.

Our answer to it

There is no second configuration. The same eleven rules, in the same words, grouped differently — no confirmation dialog, nothing lost, and nobody has to stay in Advanced. Everything remains doable in Simple. What Nielsen is right about is the failure mode, and the defence against it is that Advanced adds no capability, only a different arrangement.

There is no canvas

The stages are things a shop owner recognises, not nodes on a canvas. No wiring, no edges, nothing to draw. Vapi retired its own workflow canvas entirely in August 2026 — the node graph turned out not to be worth maintaining as a separate surface.

Tools are what make a rule real

An agent that cannot quote a price in a stage will not quote one, whatever a caller says to it. That is a different kind of promise from an agent that has been asked not to — and it is why some rules here carry a badge saying guaranteed and others say guideline.

It's containment, not a wall

Scoping tools shrinks what a hijacked conversation can reach — someone who tricks the agent during booking still can't touch a tool it was never given. The literature is consistent that this is containment rather than prevention, and we describe it that way rather than claiming immunity.

The self-inflicted failure

Contradictory rules are the most common reason an owner decides the AI is unreliable. There's no error to see; there's just an agent that answers differently on different days.

One product does this

Intercom's Fin reviews its guidance before publishing, checking for ambiguity, redundancy, contradiction and system limitations. It's the only clear precedent in the category, and it's a good one — worth copying rather than inventing.

The diff is the hard part

Plenty of platforms keep versions. Very few show what actually changed in words a person can read. Dialogflow CX is the best of them, and this is modelled on it.

A revert within a day is a signal

It usually means a change broke something and the owner noticed before we did. That's worth alerting on, and it's in the instrumentation for exactly that reason.

Flow 1.6 · atlas F-09

Agent design

Let a shop owner tell the AI how to behave — without writing a prompt, learning a flow builder, or knowing what a language model is. The atlas's central claim is that one agent should have two views rather than two modes. The research both supports that and produces the sharpest objection to it in this whole programme, so this page starts there.

01

The objection, stated properly

Jakob Nielsen, writing in 2026 about AI agent interfaces specifically, rejects Simple/Advanced toggles as a category. Two arguments: they “fracture codebases and documentation,” and they assume a population of power users that mostly doesn't exist — most people are perpetual intermediates, occasional power users rather than people who graduate to expert mode and stay there. His prescription instead is one unified design where controls keep fixed spatial positions and only what's expanded changes.

That is the most credentialed critique available of exactly what the atlas proposes, and it would be dishonest to design past it. Three responses, and one concession:

Response

There is no second configuration to fracture

His strongest objection is about duplicated logic. Here the same eleven rules, in the same words, with the same identities, are re-presented — Simple groups them by category, Advanced groups them by moment in the conversation. Nothing exists in one view that doesn't exist in the other.

Response

Advanced adds no capability

It adds visibility — which tools each stage can reach. Every rule remains editable in Simple. Nobody has to move to Advanced to do anything, which is what stops the simple view from rotting into a demo.

Response

It's a view, not a mode

No confirmation dialog, no “are you sure”, no preference that persists and quietly becomes an identity. The count of rules is printed beside the switch specifically so the losslessness is visible rather than promised.

Concession

He may still be right about who uses it

The atlas already asks (question 9.b) whether Advanced should ship in v1 at all. The honest answer after this research is: probably not. Ship Simple, instrument agent.mode_toggled, and let the first customer who asks for stages tell us it's needed. Designing it now is cheap; shipping it now is a bet on a user who may not exist.

02

The rationale I had to correct

The atlas argues that structured rules beat a prose prompt. The direction is right and one of the stated reasons is wrong.

ClaimWhat the evidence says
Structured rules are more reliable than prose because of the formatNot supported. A study across 10–160 simultaneous rules in markdown, plain text, prose and tables found markdown's advantage was ≤2.1 points for most models and negative for one — “the same format is the best performer for one model and the worst for another.” Formatting is not the win
Long rule sets degradeStrongly supported, with a number. Perfect-response rate collapses to zero by 80 rules, for every model and every format tested. The atlas's warning at 25 rules is conservative and defensible; Intercom caps its own guidance at 100 active items
Negative instructions are less reliable than positive reframesSupported for chat models. A model told to avoid a topic mentioned it 33% → 36% of the time — the instruction measurably backfired. So “give the price only from the list” genuinely does beat “never invent prices”
Contradictions produce inconsistency rather than errorsSupported, and worse than assumed. GPT-4o answered without acknowledging a contradiction 97.5% of the time; the best model surfaced it in 45% of cases. There is also a recency bias — later rules quietly win, degrading satisfaction of earlier ones by 15–25 points

So the real case for structured rules is three things, none of them formatting. They can be individually switched off, which keeps the active count low and makes a misbehaving agent bisectable. They can be checked against each other before publishing, which the model itself will not do. And some of them can stop being text at all and become structure — which is the next section.

03

Rules that are enforced, not requested

The most important idea in this flow, and the reason two badges appear on every rule.

RuleBadgeHow it is actually enforced
Give the price only from the price listGuaranteedA typed field lookup in the structured price table. Never generation. The rule is a guarantee, not a request
Never quote for a bridal partyGuaranteedIn the booking stage the pricing tool is absent when the party-size condition holds. Not an instruction — a missing capability
Get a person when the customer is upsetGuaranteedAn escalation trigger evaluated every turn, outside the model's discretion
Always confirm a booking by repeating it backGuaranteedA required step in the booking action before it commits
Be warmGuidelineGenuinely prompt-level. Style is probabilistic and the badge says so
Always be rightGuidelineAccepted, and honestly labelled. Refusing to save it would be worse than saving it with the truth attached

Tool scoping is containment, not immunity. Removing a capability from a stage genuinely shrinks what a hijacked conversation can reach — someone who manipulates the agent during booking still cannot touch a tool it was never given. But the literature is consistent that least-privilege is a baseline control rather than a prompt-injection cure, and dedicated papers on the problem were still being published through 2026. The product copy says containment, and the badge says guaranteed for this rule, never the agent cannot be manipulated.

A counter-example worth knowing. Botpress has nodes and per-node tools, and its own documentation says the model “will decide when to use these tools based on your instructions” — the scoping is instructional, not structural. Having a flow canvas does not mean having real enforcement. Whether a badge can honestly say guaranteed depends entirely on what happens underneath it.

04

The contradiction check has one precedent

Intercom's Fin ships this — an assistant that reviews the configured guidance before publishing and checks for ambiguity, redundancy, contradiction, clarity and system limitations, letting the author accept or refine each finding. It also splits escalation rules (structured conditions) from escalation guidance (natural language), which is the same instructional-versus-structural line drawn here. Nothing comparable was found in Retell, Vapi, Bland, Synthflow, ElevenLabs, Voiceflow, Botpress, Decagon, Parloa or Sierra.

And the resolution is never automatic. “Which wins when someone asks about a wedding?” is a business decision. The check finds the conflict and hands it back with both rules quoted, three ways to resolve it, and the option to publish anyway with the choice recorded — so that if the behaviour turns out odd, the first thing anyone looks at is the thing that was already flagged.

No product was found where switching between simple and advanced views of agent behaviour is confirmed lossless. That's a genuine gap rather than a solved pattern with prior art — which is worth knowing in both directions: it means the idea is distinctive, and it means nobody has proven it works.

06

Version history, and the detail worth stealing

Dialogflow CX is the reference implementation: immutable snapshots, a versions pane with timestamped entries, and — rare in this category — a human-readable diff of the state before and after each change. Retell versions agents but publishes no diff.

The one detail worth copying outright: in Dialogflow, restoring over unsaved changes automatically creates a version of the current state first, so you can revert a revert. That turns the safety net into something with no trapdoor in it, and it costs almost nothing to implement. It's in this design for that reason.

One honest caveat: the claim that revert-availability increases willingness to edit is consistent with established interface theory — Shneiderman's sixth rule, permit easy reversal of actions, is justified explicitly on anxiety-reduction grounds — but no empirical study measuring it for agent configuration was found. It's a well-grounded hypothesis, not a proven number.

07

Writing rules in Kinyarwanda

Atlas question 9.d asks whether the contradiction check can run in Kinyarwanda with acceptable accuracy. The honest answer is that nobody knows. Instruction-following degrades for lower-resourced languages as a general finding, and there is a matched academic study for Basque — but no Kinyarwanda-specific figure exists publicly, and the closest Swahili paper was inaccessible. This is a genuine gap in the literature rather than a risk somebody has already measured.

The design response is not to guess. Write the same ten rules in Kinyarwanda and in English, run both against the same set of test calls, and compare the agent's behaviour. That is a week of work and it settles a question no paper can answer for us. Until it is settled, rules authored in Kinyarwanda are stored as written and the contradiction check runs on both the original and an English rendering, with any disagreement between the two treated as a finding rather than silently resolved.

Two rules cannot be removed, and the lock is enforced in the product rather than in policy. The AI disclosure, because it is becoming law in several markets and is simply the right way to answer somebody's phone. And the out-of-scope deflection, because WhatsApp's terms prohibit general-purpose assistants — an owner must not be able to configure an agent that answers arbitrary questions, since that would put their own WhatsApp account at risk, not ours.
