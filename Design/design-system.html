Foundations · v1.0 · September 2026

A design system for software that answers the phone.

Subiza answers calls and messages for businesses that cannot afford anyone to sit by the phone. That single fact decides everything below: the interface is read on a mid-range Android in a shop, over a connection that is sometimes 2G, by someone who is simultaneously serving a customer in front of them. It is not read at leisure on a 27-inch monitor.

So this system is built line-first and text-first. It spends its weight on legibility, on touch targets, and on saying what is happening — not on shadows, gradients or illustration. Every token, component and rule here is traceable to a decision in the Flow Atlas, and every one of them is written to work at 360 pixels before it is allowed to work at 1440.

01 — Foundations

Six principles, each with a cost attached

A principle that costs nothing is a slogan. Each of these forbids something we would otherwise have done.

1. The line is the material

Structure is drawn with 1px hairlines, not shadows. Four elevation levels exist and three of them have no shadow at all.
Costs us: no soft card stacks, no floating aesthetic. Everything sits flat and reads fast on a cheap LCD in daylight.

2. Lime is a surface, never a voice

The signature colour is a fill with near-black on top. It is never a text colour, never a link colour, never an icon on white.
Costs us: the easy branded-link look. Lime on paper is 1.2:1 — it would be decoration masquerading as information.

3. Say the state, don't imply it

Connected, degraded, listening, out of credit — each has a named visual state with a word beside it. Status is never colour alone; the shape of the dot changes too.
Costs us: density. Labels take room we would rather give to content.

4. Designed at 360, then grown

Every component's first form is the small one. Where a component cannot survive the trip it is replaced, not squeezed — the rail becomes a tab bar, the table becomes a list, the modal becomes a sheet.
Costs us: two designs for four components instead of one clever one.

5. Weight is a feature

No chart library, no icon font, no animation runtime. Charts are hand-drawn SVG; the waveform is six divs; the sprite is inline.
Costs us: convenience. Every dependency would have to earn its kilobytes against a data bundle that costs a real share of someone's month.

6. Four languages, one layout

Kinyarwanda runs 15–25% longer than English. Nothing is sized to fit English; buttons wrap, labels have room, no text is truncated to make a grid work.
Costs us: tight, satisfying alignment. We take the looser one that survives translation.

02 — Foundations

Colour

One signature, one ink, three status families and a deep panel. That is the whole palette. Every value below carries its measured contrast ratio against the surface it is designed to sit on, computed with the WCAG 2.x relative-luminance formula.

On APCA. The newer perceptual contrast algorithm is not normative — WCAG 3 remains a draft and APCA was not adopted into it. Every ratio quoted here is WCAG 2.1/2.2, because that is what Rwandan and EU procurement will actually test against.

03 — Foundations

Typography

Three families with three jobs. Bricolage Grotesque is the voice — a variable grotesque with a genuine optical-size axis, so a 48px headline is drawn with tighter apertures than a 16px one instead of being the same shape scaled. Plus Jakarta Sans carries the reading, with the tall x-height and open counters that survive a low-density panel. JetBrains Mono is for the things that are literally strings: phone numbers, dial codes, amounts, timestamps, token names.

Inter and Space Grotesk are excluded by name. Both are excellent and both are so heavily used in this category that they carry no signal at all — a Subiza screen set in Inter looks like every other AI product's screen. Distinctiveness is a functional requirement for a brand nobody has heard of yet.

Radius: three steps, used for three different meanings

Uniform rounding is the fastest way to make an interface look generated. Each step here means something.

4px — data

Inputs, chips, tags, table cells. Things that hold literal values stay sharp.

12px — objects

Cards, banners, sheets, modals. Things you pick up and move around.

24px — moments

The hero, the live call card, media. Reserved. If everything is 24px, nothing is.

Plus a pill, which is not a fourth step but a shape: buttons, chips and meters are pills because a pill reads as pressable in a way a rounded rectangle does not.

05 — Foundations

Depth

Four levels. Three of them contain no shadow. An element is only allowed a shadow if it is genuinely floating over content the user was just looking at — which is true of menus, toasts and modals, and of nothing else.

0 — flat

Fills. No border, no shadow.

1 — hairline

Cards, rows, tiles. The default.

2 — raised

Toggle knobs, chat bubbles.

3–4 — floating

Menus, toasts, modals, sheets.

Why lines beat shadows here. A soft shadow relies on the display resolving a 4% alpha gradient. On the TN and low-end IPS panels this product will mostly be read on — often at low brightness to save battery — that gradient collapses to nothing and the card loses its edge entirely. A 1px hairline at 9% alpha survives every panel, every brightness and every angle.

Motion in Subiza does exactly two jobs: it shows where a thing came from, and it shows that something is alive. It never decorates and it never delays. The ceiling is 300ms — beyond that a transition stops feeling like physics and starts feeling like waiting.

The reduced-motion contract. Under prefers-reduced-motion: reduce every duration collapses to 0.01ms and every looping animation stops — including the waveform and the live dot. Those two are the ones a designer is tempted to exempt because they carry meaning. They do not get an exemption: the meaning is carried by the word "On a call now" beside the dot, which is why that word exists.

Do
transform · opacity
Animate only compositor properties. Everything in this system animates transform, opacity, colour or a single dimension.
Never
height · top · margin
Layout-triggering animation drops frames on the exact devices this product targets. Use a scaled wrapper or a grid-rows transition instead.
07 — Foundations

Icons

One set: Lucide, ISC-licensed, no attribution required, 1,600+ glyphs. Inlined as an SVG sprite of 176 symbols — no icon font, no runtime, no network request. Stroke is set to 1.75 rather than the shipped 2, because at 16px a 2px stroke fills the counters and the glyph turns into a blob.

No stock illustration. At all. The obvious move for a young product is a set of flat vector people from unDraw or Storyset. We researched it and rejected it: ElevenLabs, Sierra, Retell and Vapi all use zero stock vector people, because the moment a reader recognises the illustration pack, the product looks assembled rather than built. Subiza's visual interest comes from its own instruments — the waveform, the transcript, the dial-code card, the coverage ring. Those are further down this page, and they are things no other product could use.

09 — Components

Buttons

Five variants, three sizes, and one rule that outranks all of them: one lime button per screen. The lime is the answer to "what do I do here" — a second one turns it into a question.

Loading keeps the label's width. The text goes transparent and a spinner sits on top, so the button never changes size. A button that shrinks to "…" moves everything beside it — and on a phone, moves it under the thumb that is already descending.

Below 560px, buttons in a row become a stack, and each becomes full-width. Two 44px targets side by side on a 360px screen leaves about 150px each with a gap of 12 — comfortably inside the mis-tap zone for a thumb. This is a redesign, not a reflow: the visual hierarchy is re-expressed vertically, primary first.

11 — Components

Forms

Inputs are 48px tall — above the 44px minimum, because the person filling this in is often standing up. Labels sit above the field, always visible; there are no placeholder-as-label fields anywhere in this product.

This is what callers will hear in the greeting.

Keep this number. Subiza never replaces it.

Sunday is missing. Callers on Sunday will be told you're closed — is that right?

Expires in 0:24. Send it again

The OTP looks like six boxes and is not six boxes. Six separate inputs is the obvious build and it is wrong: a paste fills only the first box, iOS autofill has no way to distribute one code across six controls, and a screen reader announces six unrelated fields instead of one code. The WCAG 2.2 SC 3.3.8 Understanding document names exactly this pattern as a failure example, and the GOV.UK Design System uses a single field for the same reason. So this component is one <input> with autocomplete="one-time-code", laid over six painted slots that are aria-hidden. Paste works, autofill works, backspace works, and the caret is drawn into the active slot so it still reads as six boxes.

It is also the least forgiving input in the product. WhatsApp's Embedded Signup code has a 30-second time-to-live and the owner must type it themselves — hence the visible countdown, the oversized slots, and a resend that appears before the code expires rather than after.

Choice cards

A radio group where the options carry real consequence. Never a native radio: the label is the target, the whole card is 100+ pixels of it.

15 — Product

The live call card

Subiza's signature object. It appears at the top of Home whenever a call is in progress, and it is the one place in the product where a dark panel, a 24px radius and a looping animation are all permitted at once — because it is the one place where something is genuinely happening right now.

The waveform is twenty-eight <i> elements and one keyframe. No canvas, no audio API, no library, no per-frame JavaScript. Each bar has a fixed base height and a staggered delay, so the shape reads as speech rather than as a metronome — and the whole thing costs under 300 bytes and zero main-thread work. It is not a real-time visualisation of the audio, and it does not pretend to be: it is an alive indicator, and the truthful information — duration, caller, topic — sits in text beside it.

At 360px the two actions stack and the caller's history line drops. "Take the call" is the only thing that matters when a customer is actually on the line, so it gets the full width and the top position, and nothing above it competes.

18 — Product

The forwarding code

The most consequential 20 characters in the product. It is a GSM supplementary-service string the owner dials on their own handset, and it is what makes Subiza work without asking a carrier for anything. It gets its own component, its own typography and its own verification.

Dial this on the phone with the SIM
**61*250788456123*11*20#

This sends calls to Subiza only after your phone has rung for 20 seconds. Your phone still rings first, every time.

Android only. It opens the dialler pre-filled and never dials by itself. On iPhone this button is replaced by copy-and-paste steps.

We checked — it's working

We called your number and Subiza picked up after 20 seconds. Verified 2 minutes ago.

To switch it off later
##61#

Calls go straight back to your phone. Nothing else changes and your number is untouched.

Copy is the primary action, and that is a correction. This component originally led with a tap-to-dial button, which is wrong: on iPhone, MMI codes inside a tel: link fail silently — Apple has acknowledged the bug across several releases and not fixed it — and on Android the link only ever pre-fills the dialler, never dials, a restriction that dates to a 2012 exploit in which a crafted tel: link could factory-reset a Samsung handset from a web page. A primary button that does nothing on half the devices is worse than no button, so copy leads everywhere and the dialler shortcut is an Android convenience.

Only the number is highlighted, and the rest of the string is left plain. The owner does not need to understand **61* or *11*20# — they need to be able to check the one part that is theirs. Highlighting the whole string would be decoration; highlighting nothing would leave twenty undifferentiated symbols to be mistyped. The mono face and the word-break guarantee it never truncates, at any width, in any language.

The off-switch is shown at the same moment as the on-switch. Handing someone a code that changes how their phone behaves, without also handing them the way back, is how a product loses a customer's trust in the first ten minutes. It also happens to be the single strongest answer to the objection this product will hear most often — "what if I want to stop?"

The reassurance line is part of the component, not an afterthought. Running out of credit is the moment a prepaid customer is most likely to churn, and the fear is not the cost — it is that their business goes silent. Stating the fallback inside the low-credit state converts the scariest screen in the product into a demonstration that the foundational promise holds: Subiza never makes a business less reachable than it was before.

Every chart in this product is hand-written SVG or flexbox. Chart.js is ~200KB, Recharts pulls React and D3. 22 — Law

The eight states

Flow 24 of the atlas sets an acceptance criterion: every screen has all eight of these, and a screenshot audit proves it. A screen that has only been designed in its happy state is a screen whose other states will be discovered by a customer.

#StateRequired behaviourComponent
1LoadingA skeleton shaped like the real content. Never an unexplained spinner past 3 seconds — after that, words.sk

Nothing in this system is considered designed until all eight exist. The states are not variants of the screen — they are the screen, seven-eighths of the time that anything is wrong.

23 — Law

Responsive: what gets redesigned, not reflowed

The base design width is 360px, which is the modal Android width in this market and roughly the narrowest phone still in wide use. Everything wider is an enhancement. Four breakpoints, and the honest list of what changes at each.

The redesign register

Six components in this system stop being themselves on a small screen. Each is listed with what it becomes and why the reflow was refused.

Wide formSmall formWhy not just reflow it
Sidebar rail, 7 itemsBottom tab bar, 4 + More, frequency-ordered, shortened labels, dot instead of countA hamburger hides the product. The thumb arc is at the bottom of the screen, not the top-left corner

Container queries, not only media queries. Roughly 95% of browsers now support container-type, and the stat card, the channel tile and the conversation row all query their own slot rather than the window — so the same component is correct in a 240px sidebar and in a 900px page without anyone passing it a size prop. The remaining 5% get the wide form, which is never broken, only less clever. No feature is behind that query.

Touch targets are 48×48 CSS pixels with at least 8px between them — above the 44px WCAG 2.2 minimum, because this interface is frequently used one-handed while doing something else. Where a glyph is visually smaller, the hit area is padded out to 48 regardless.
