Escalation & the rota

Getting a customer to a human without them ever feeling abandoned — and keeping a promise the product made on the owner's behalf. Fourteen screens, and half of them belong to the staff member rather than the customer.

01

Not the failure path

Atlas §1: “An AI that traps a frustrated customer in a loop is worse than a phone that rings out, because at least a ringing phone does not pretend to help. Escalation is not the failure path. It is the feature that makes the rest safe to deploy.”

So it is built as a primitive, not an error handler: its own triggers, its own routing order, its own promise with a deadline, its own loop breaker, its own band of acceptable frequency. And golive.html already enforces the consequence — an agent with no escalation target is one of only two things that block going live.

02

Half this file is Aline's screen, not the customer's

Atlas §2's third principle is the one that shapes the build: “The handover experience is designed for the human receiving it, not just for the customer. A7 the staff member is the actor most able to quietly kill adoption from inside.”

She does not kill it by complaining. She kills it by being handed a cold call, feeling foolish in front of a customer, and quietly starting to work around it. So e5 is her screen and it carries four things: who is on the line, what they want, why she was fetched, and — the part nobody builds — what the agent has already told them.

That last list has a negative half, and it is the important one. It did not say the slot is free. It gave no price for the child. Knowing what the machine did not commit to is what stops Aline contradicting it in front of Marie — which is the specific humiliation that turns a staff member against a tool for good.

03

Two triggers that are not settings

Nine triggers; seven are hers. Two are drawn as locked controls in the same grammar as the withdrawal control in voice.html and the priority rows in language.html — present, legible, and they do not move.

Locked triggerWhy it cannot be switched off
“I want a person”An ethical floor, and the single thing that makes callers tolerate an AI at all. Honoured immediately — including during the greeting, with no round of “let me try to help first”
Anything out of scopeW8. WhatsApp forbids general-purpose assistants on business accounts, so deflection is a condition of being allowed to operate there — not our preference

The greeting case deserves its own sentence. Products that attempt one round of AI help before honouring an explicit request for a human are the reason people distrust automated phone systems in the first place. SC 15.2 allows at most one turn between request and escalation; the design allows zero.

04

Containment is a band, and both edges are alarms

SC 15.8 targets 70–80% of conversations resolved without escalation, and the atlas flags that “containment that is too high is as much a warning as one that is too low.” So e1 renders it as a band with a marker, not a gauge climbing toward 100%. 95% would not be a triumph; it would mean the agent is answering things it should be bringing to her. Both edges are drawn, and the copy says so.

05

The promise, and the part nobody builds

Mode three — message and promise — is the commonest path for a one-person business and the one that has to be excellent. Two decisions make it work.

The time is chosen, not typed. There is no free-text field anywhere in this flow, because a free-text field is how “as soon as possible” gets written. The owner picks from two real times. A promise with a time can be kept and checked; one without can be neither.

And the product chases it. Atlas §5.3 step 6 — “If the deadline approaches unmet, the owner is reminded… the part nobody builds and it is the whole point” — is drawn as four stages, visible from the moment the promise is made:

WhenWhat happens
On captureWhatsApp to the owner, and the item goes to the top of needs you with the deadline on its face
One hour beforeOne reminder, naming the customer and what she wanted — never “you have tasks”
At the named timeA last reminder. After this point it is not the owner who is late, it is us
Fifteen minutes afterThe customer is written to with an apology and a new specific time

A decision the atlas leaves open, taken here. The atlas says the apology is sent proactively; it does not say whether the owner approves it. Sending a message in her name without asking is a real intrusion — but requiring approval means the commonest outcome is silence, which is exactly the failure this exists to prevent. So it is automatic, visible, and stoppable for fifteen minutes. If she does nothing it goes, because doing nothing is the decision that breaks a promise and the design should not collaborate with it.

06

The 45-second cap, drawn

Ring-and-brief is capped at 45 seconds and the cap is on screen, counting. A queue that states its own limit is the whole difference between this and every phone system the caller has previously met. Past the cap we fall through to a message rather than testing her patience — and the hold copy is an honest wait, not “one moment please”.

Atlas 15.c asks whether 45 seconds is right for this market, where callers may be patient with a person and impatient with a machine. This file does not settle that; it makes the number visible so it can be argued with.

10

Redesigned for 360, not reflowed

Every key/value row in this file — Aline's briefing, the captured message, the silent-takeover log — puts its label above the value below 640 rather than keeping an 84px label column. In Kinyarwanda those labels run long and the value would wrap to three words a line, which is unreadable at exactly the moment somebody is reading it with a customer on the line.

The four stages of a promise wrap their timestamp to a third line at the text indent rather than shrinking the type. The promise actions go full width one per row; two 40px buttons side by side at 360 is two buttons nobody can hit while holding a phone.

And the shell's small-screen override is restated, not inherited — the trap recorded in conversations.html §07. A component's class rules travel when lifted; its @media block does not.

11

Open questions this file does not settle

Atlas 15.a — whether the telephony provider supports warm transfer with a spoken brief at all, or whether ring-and-brief is the practical ceiling — is unresolved and material: e3 may not be buildable on day one, in which case e4 is the product. Both are drawn for that reason. 15.b, the right frustration threshold for Kinyarwanda speech, is a model question this design cannot answer; the trigger is drawn with no numeric dial precisely because we would be inventing the number. 15.d — whether the caller should be told a human is being fetched or simply experience it — is answered for voice (told, because they are about to hear silence) and for chat (not told, because there is nothing to explain).

And the standing caveat: the Kinyarwanda here is carefully written and has not been reviewed by a native speaker. Atlas SC 6.12 requires that walkthrough. The spoken lines in e4 matter most — they are the only copy in this programme a customer hears rather than reads.
