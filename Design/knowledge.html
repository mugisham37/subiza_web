We don't actually know how fast this goes stale

There's no published research on how quickly a small business's own knowledge drifts out of date, or whether reminding people helps. This nudge is a reasonable guess, and we'll measure it on real tenants rather than pretend otherwise.

Kinyarwanda isn't supported by the mainstream speech engines. Business names, place names and people's names get mangled by default, using English or French approximations — so this isn't an advanced setting, it's part of the main flow.

Knowledge is the boundary

This isn't a filing cabinet. It defines what the agent can answer at all — which is also how WhatsApp's prohibition on general-purpose assistants is honoured structurally rather than by policy. There is nothing outside this to answer from.

Two layers, and the difference is the point

Prices, hours, location and policies are typed fields — read, never composed. Everything else is retrieved from text with a source attached. A question the first layer can answer is always answered by it.

The actual gap in the market

Not one product in this category treats a price list as structured data. They all flatten it into text. That is where invented prices come from, and quoting a wrong price is the single most damaging thing this product could do to a small business.

Numbers fail differently

This isn't general vagueness. Research shows models systematically favour certain digits and misstate figures even when the correct one is in the context they were given. Numeric error is its own failure mode with its own shape — which is why the answer is to stop generating numbers, not to prompt more carefully.

Question-and-answer beats a document

Long documents get chopped into chunks, and those boundaries routinely cut through the middle of a fact — which is a known cause of retrieval failure. A question and its answer is already a whole unit. There is no head-to-head study proving this, and we'd rather say so than pretend.

The agent knows what it couldn't do

Every question it couldn't answer is recorded. Three of the same one becomes a task rather than a nudge. Intercom's Fin is the only comparable product we found doing this properly — and the detail worth copying is showing the actual conversations underneath, not just a count.

A pattern, not a proven number

The pattern is shipped by real products, but no independent measurement of its effect on resolution exists — every figure we found came from a vendor's own marketing. We use it because the logic is sound, and we'll measure it ourselves.

We never let the model arbitrate

When two sources disagree, general models choose badly and inconsistently — on a benchmark built from conflicting evidence, the best model reached about 34%. So the typed table wins, always, and that precedence is hard-coded rather than reasoned about.

And we say what we ignored

We found no product anywhere that tells a user which source was overridden. Hiding it would be easier — but an owner whose website says something different from their agent deserves to learn it from us.

Mangling the business's own name

No other quality problem embarrasses a business the way this does. An agent that mispronounces the name of the shop it represents undermines everything else it gets right — which is why this is in the main flow with a play button, not behind a settings menu.

What we found about Kinyarwanda

Amazon Polly doesn't list it. Azure doesn't list it. Google almost certainly doesn't either. A Rwanda-focused provider called Proto does — with government and NGO deployments in Kinyarwanda — which makes them the first conversation to have, as a supplier or as a competitor.

Flow 1.7 · atlas F-10

Knowledge base

Give the agent the facts about the business in a form it can answer from reliably, and let the owner see, correct and trust what it knows. The atlas's central claim — that prices should be structured data, never generated — turns out to be the best-evidenced idea in this whole programme. The transparency mechanism attached to it turns out to be the shakiest.

01

The claim that the research strongly supports

Table-Augmented Generation, CIDR 2025. On a benchmark of natural-language questions over a database: Text2SQL 17%, retrieval-plus-ranking 2%, plain RAG over text 0%, and reading from structured data 55% — a 20 to 65 point spread. Their questions are harder than “what does a haircut cost”, so this isn't proof that retrieval fails on simple lookups. But it is direct evidence that free-text retrieval degrades catastrophically on exactly the class of question a price is.

FindingWhy it matters here
Numbers fail differently from prose. A factual-hallucination benchmark found error rates of 48–82%, worst in numeric domains; on census data “all models display scores of 1 … a high rate of generating incorrect numbers”Prompting more carefully does not fix a class of error that has its own mechanism
The failure is systematic, not noise. Models over-produce the digit 1 and under-produce 8 and 9, tracking a distribution learned in pretraining, traced to digit-selective neurons in the final layersA wrong price will not look random. It will look plausible, which is worse
Having the right answer in context is not enough. Hallucination leaderboards measure exactly this — models summarising a retrieved document they were handed, still getting it wrong“We gave it the price list” is not a guarantee. Reading a typed cell is

So the rule is: a price is a table cell, not a sentence. The agent reads it out; it never composes one. A price that isn't in the table is never spoken — the agent fetches a person. That is the single highest-consequence hallucination class in this product, and it is removed by construction rather than mitigated by instruction.

02

The uncomfortable finding about citations

Atlas question 10.e asks whether per-answer citation is meaningful to a shop owner or an engineer's idea of transparency. The research has an answer, and it is not the flattering one.

Citations raise trust even when they are random. In a study accepted to AAAI-39: citations significantly increased trust in AI answers even when the citations were irrelevant; one citation and five citations produced comparable trust; and users who actually clicked through and verified a citation reported lower trust afterwards. Once verified, random citations were trusted no more than no citation at all.

Read plainly: a citation is a trust signal that most people never check. It makes an answer feel more reliable regardless of whether it is. That is close to the opposite of what a transparency feature is supposed to do, and it means a citation cannot be counted as a safety mechanism.

So we changed what it is

The citation is an edit affordance

Not a badge. It's a button that jumps to the exact row and puts the cursor in it. Its job is to make a wrong price fixable in two taps — a use case the trust research doesn't cover and one that is unambiguously useful.

And we added what does work

A hedge, where hedging is honest

Separate research found that low-confidence, hedged language is one of the few things that reliably makes people push back on an AI answer — more so than a source link. So when the ask box answers from outside the structured layer, it says so in words rather than decorating the answer with a source.

What nobody has tested. No study exists on this specific format — “from your price list, row 3” — shown to non-expert small-business owners. It remains a design bet. The difference is that it is now a bet on editability, which is checkable, rather than on trust, which the evidence says would be misplaced.

03

When two sources disagree

General models arbitrate badly. On a benchmark built from genuinely conflicting evidence, the best model reached about 34% exact match, and standard retrieval systems “either arbitrarily choose an answer or use parametric knowledge to break ties.” There is no principled precedence rule inside the model, so the product must supply one.

RuleConsequence
Typed data always beats retrieved textHard-coded. The model never chooses between them
The owner is told which source was ignoredWith both values shown, and one tap to fix either side
Newer never beats structuredA website updated yesterday still loses to a price table edited last month. Recency is not authority

No product was found that does the second row. Telling a user which source was overridden appears to be undocumented anywhere in the category. It is also the difference between a system that quietly resolves a contradiction and one that hands an owner a fact about their own business they didn't know — namely that their website is wrong.

04

Gaps mined from real calls

The strongest re-engagement mechanic in the product, because it is a specific, evidenced, two-minute task derived from the owner's own customers rather than a generic prompt.

Design choiceWhere it comes from
Show the actual conversations underneath the countIntercom Fin's content suggestions put source conversations in a side drawer. A count is a statistic; three quoted customers asking about parking is a task
Accept, edit or reject each oneSame precedent — with a diff view before anything goes live
Rank by how many times it was askedThree occurrences is a gap. One is a curiosity, and the UI says so rather than treating both as urgent
Dismissing is a first-class actionAn owner who doesn't want to answer a question should be able to say so once and never see it again

Honest about the evidence. The pattern is shipped by Intercom and Guru; Zendesk leaves it to third parties. But every quantified claim about its effect — deflection lifts of 20–30%, “3–5× more gaps than manual audits” — traces back to a single vendor's own marketing with undisclosed methodology. There is no independent measurement. We build it because the logic holds, not because someone proved it.

The respelling approach is real and non-technical. ElevenLabs' own documentation tells users to “write a word more phonetically,” using capitals and dashes for stress, rather than learning a phonetic alphabet. That is exactly the interface this screen offers — write it as it sounds, press play, try again. The mechanism is proven on languages these engines support.

And here is the risk, stated rather than buried. Whether any Kinyarwanda-capable engine exposes an override at all is unpublished. This screen is designed against a capability we have not confirmed exists on the provider we will actually use. It is a question to put to Proto — or to whoever else — before this ships, not after. If the answer is no, the pronunciation dictionary becomes a feature request to a supplier rather than a screen in our product.
