Why forwarding, not a new number

No porting, no regulatory bundle, no waiting, and the number on your shop sign and your business cards keeps working. But there's a better reason than any of those.

It makes the product better

Conditional forwarding means every call Subiza handles is, by definition, a call the business would otherwise have lost. That's the number that renews the subscription — and it falls out of the setup choice rather than being estimated.

Three codes, not one

No answer, busy and unreachable are three separate conditions on the carrier's switch, each with its own code. Most businesses want all three, so we present it as one choice and walk you through the three dials.

Twenty seconds, fixed

The standard allows anywhere from 5 to 30 seconds. We picked 20 and fixed it, because every extra variable in the string is another thing a network can reject — and a rejected code costs far more than a perfectly-tuned ring time gains.

The one place we genuinely cannot help

There is no API that sets call forwarding on an arbitrary GSM handset. Not one we haven't built — one that does not exist. So the goal here isn't automation; it's making a fifteen-second manual action feel trivial and get rewarded immediately.

Why copy comes first

On Android, a tel: link opens the dialler with the code in it — it never dials by itself, and it hasn't since a 2012 exploit let a web page silently factory-reset Samsung phones. On iPhone, MMI codes in tel: links fail silently; Apple has acknowledged it and not fixed it. So copy is the action that works everywhere, and the dialler shortcut is offered as a convenience, not as the path.

This replaces your voicemail

Carrier voicemail is implemented with exactly this mechanism. A line can hold one destination per condition, so registering ours silently replaces theirs. Nobody warns people about this. We do, before they dial, because finding out later feels like something was taken.

Where we're ahead

Every comparable product we looked at asks the customer to call themselves to check. Not one of them verifies it. We place the call, watch where it lands, and tell you.

It's also the only mechanism available: there is no operator API anywhere in this market that can be asked whether forwarding is on.

Why “don't pick up”

No-answer forwarding only fires when the phone rings out. If you pick up, there's nothing to observe — which is why “you answered” is a neutral result with a friendly retry, not a red error.

Four outcomes, not two

It worked. You answered. It didn't divert. It diverted but the caller's number was lost. That last one is a pass with a caveat, and treating it as a failure would be a lie — the product works, one feature doesn't.

We never make it your fault

“Invalid MMI code” appears regularly on codes that are entirely correct — it comes from the handset or the switch. Our copy says so, because telling someone they got it wrong when they didn't is how you lose them at the last step.

The order matters

Shorter code, then look at what's already there, then clear everything, then a person. Each step is cheaper than the next, and the last one is always a human — because a rescue here costs us less than a lost customer.

Some lines simply can't

Some prepaid plans don't permit call forwarding at all. When that's the answer, a new Subiza number is the fix — and we say so rather than making someone try the same code five times.

The undo code is shown up front

Someone who knows how to reverse a change is far more willing to make it. Hiding the exit doesn't increase retention; it increases hesitation. So ##002# is on this screen, in Settings, and in the connection summary, permanently.

The silent failure is the dangerous one

A business that believes it's covered and isn't is worse off than one we never connected. Weekly re-verification is cheap and it's the only defence, because forwarding is state held by someone else that can disappear with no signal at all.

Honest about waiting

Number provisioning is programmatic but gated on regulatory approval, which is not instant in this region and often involves a human on the other side. Promising instant and delivering four days is how trust is lost in week one.

How we knew

We call your number once a week at a quiet hour and watch where it lands. It's a small cost, and it is the only method available — no operator in this market exposes an API that could simply be asked.

What we didn't do

We didn't quietly note it in a dashboard. The message reached you on WhatsApp at eight in the morning, before the shop opened — because the only thing worse than this failing is you finding out from a customer.

Flow 1.4 · atlas F-07

Phone connection

Guidance plus verification, never an action we perform. This is the step with the most external dependency and the least control in the entire product, and the design is shaped almost entirely by things that are true about GSM networks rather than things we chose.

01

What I got wrong, three times

The design system, the public site and the activation flow all showed “Open my dialler” as the primary lime button on the forwarding-code card. That was wrong, and all four surfaces are now corrected.

PlatformWhat actually happens with tel:**61*…#
Android / ChromeOpens the dialler pre-filled. It never auto-dials — the user still taps call. # should be percent-encoded as %23, since an unencoded # is a URI fragment delimiter and risks being stripped before the intent is even built. Works most of the time; an Android 14 regression in this area is still open on Google's tracker
iOS / SafariFails, silently. A regression in iOS 15.4 broke MMI codes in tel: links entirely; Apple's own DTS confirmed it with “no known workaround”. It was fixed in 15.5 and the same class of failure has since returned — a 2026 Apple developer forum thread reports two iPhones on identical iOS versions behaving differently, percent-encoding making no difference, and Apple again confirming it as a bug with no fix. There is no error the user can see or act on

So copy is the primary action and the dialler shortcut is a convenience. On Android the tel: button stays, labelled honestly. On iPhone it is replaced by three numbered steps — copy, open Phone, paste — because a button that silently does nothing is worse than no button. The prototype's handset switch in the toolbar shows both.

The reason no platform will auto-dial is a fourteen-year-old exploit. In 2012 a crafted tel: link — deliverable by web page, SMS, QR code or NFC tag — could silently push a factory-reset USSD code into Samsung's dialler with no confirmation. iPhones were spared because Apple's dialler always showed a prompt first. Every “the user must tap call” behaviour you see today descends from that.

02

The code, field by field

From 3GPP TS 22.030 §6.5.2, which defines the MMI structure, and Annex B, which lists the service codes.

PartMeaningNote
**Registration — store the destination and enable it togetherA single * also registers when a number is present, but ** is unambiguous, which is why every carrier document uses it
61Call forwarding on no reply21 unconditional · 67 busy · 62 unreachable · 002 all · 004 all conditional
*250788456123SIA — the forwarded-to numberThe only part of the string that belongs to the tenant
*11SIB — basic service group, voice telephonyOptional in isolation, structurally required here. The fields are positional, so the timer cannot be reached without it
*20#SIC — the no-reply timer, in secondsThe spec allows 5–30 in steps of five

The timer is fixed at 20 seconds and not exposed. Every additional variable in the string is another thing a handset or a switch can reject, and MMI rejection is the dominant failure here. A perfectly-tuned ring time is worth far less than a code that registers first time.

03

Rejection is common, and it is not the user's fault

“Invalid MMI code” and “Connection problem” appear regularly on syntactically correct forwarding strings, across Samsung's own community forum, Google's Android support forum and carrier forums. The causes are handset dialler quirks, switch-side rejection, and occasionally plan restrictions — none of which the person holding the phone can see or influence.

Consequence

The copy never blames

“Your phone said Invalid MMI code. That message comes from the handset or the network, not from us, and it does not mean you typed it wrong.”

Consequence

Alternates are one tap away

The shorter form without the timer and basic-service fields, an interrogation to see what's already registered, and an erase-all before retrying. In that order, cheapest first.

Consequence

A human is always the last rung

A call-back within fifteen minutes. This is the step most likely to defeat a non-technical owner, and a rescue here is cheap next to a lost customer.

Consequence

Some lines simply can't

Prepaid plans that don't permit forwarding at all exist. When that's the answer we say it, and a new Subiza number becomes the route — rather than a fifth attempt at the same code.

04

Registering ours silently replaces their voicemail

Carrier voicemail is implemented with exactly this mechanism — conditional forwarding to the voicemail platform's number. A line holds one destination per condition, so registration overwrites whatever was there, with no warning from the network and no separate confirmation.

Nobody in this category warns about it. We warn before they dial, and we offer *#61# so they can see what's registered first. Discovering a week later that voicemail quietly stopped working — and correctly blaming us for it — is a far more expensive outcome than a sentence of honest friction.

05

Verification is the whole design, and it is a differentiator

We place a call to the owner's numberFrom a verification line, while they watch the screen
They let it ring outStated plainly and repeated — no-answer forwarding only fires on a ring-out
We observe where it lands, and whether caller ID survivedBoth recorded on the tenant
Four outcomes, each with its own screenWorking · you answered · didn't divert · diverted without caller ID

Every comparable product we examined asks the customer to test it themselves. Booked By Gordon: “ring your salon number from your own mobile.” SkipCalls: “call your business number from another phone.” My AI Front Desk and RealVoice offer no verification at all. Server-initiated verification appears to be genuinely absent from this category — which is surprising, because it is cheap to build and it is the difference between an act of faith and an observed fact.

And it is the only mechanism available. CAMARA's Call Forwarding Signal API — which would let a server simply ask whether forwarding is active on an MSISDN — exists, but sits at v0.4.0 in “Initial / Incubating” status, and the Open Gateway deployments in Africa are South African and cover number verification and SIM swap, not this. There is no evidence of it on MTN or Airtel Rwanda, and no reason to expect it soon.

06

What the research settled, and what it didn't

Atlas open questionWhere it now stands
7.a — do MTN and Airtel Rwanda honour these codes?Partly answered. MTN Rwanda's own support account has publicly instructed a customer to dial #21# and ##002# — direct evidence their live network implements the standard codes. Registration forms are inferred from that, not confirmed. Nothing at all found for Airtel Rwanda. Still needs a live SIM on both networks
7.b — does caller ID survive?Unpublished. GSM signalling is designed to pass it, and some operator configurations substitute the diverting number instead. This is exactly why the verification call records it per tenant rather than assuming
7.c — who pays for the forwarded leg?Principle known, rate unknown. The diverting subscriber pays, as though they placed the call. No MTN or Airtel Rwanda tariff for it is published. The connection summary states the principle and says plainly that we haven't measured the rate — the atlas is explicit that an undisclosed carrier charge would be a serious breach of trust
7.d — is there a self-care portal we could guide them to instead?Mostly no. MTN Rwanda's MyMTN app lists no call-forwarding feature. Airtel Rwanda's *456# self-care menu might, and nobody has published its tree — five minutes on a live Airtel SIM would settle it, and if it does, that's a materially gentler path for half our users
7.e — the real ring timeout?Unpublished for Rwanda. 20–25 seconds is the common default elsewhere. We register 20 explicitly rather than relying on a default we can't see
7.f — is weekly re-verification right?Still open, and now clearly the only option: there is no API to ask, so a call is the only way to know
