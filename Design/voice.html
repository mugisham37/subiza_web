It says your name

Every preview says your real greeting with your real business name, not a demo sentence. Hearing "Muraho, ni Salon Ubwiza" in a natural voice is the first moment the product stops being an idea — and it costs nothing.

Kinyarwanda has no commercial voice

The mainstream engines do not speak Kinyarwanda; ours comes from the project's own language programme. Where we haven't reached the quality bar we say so and offer the nearest option — we never quietly substitute a French-accented approximation. The first caller would notice, and the trust would be gone.

Whose voice is a legal question, not a form field

The account owner asks. The voice owner agrees. And the voice owner alone can withdraw — even after leaving the business, even over the owner's objection. There is no control anywhere in this product that lets the account owner change that, and that single fact shapes every screen after this one.

We refuse the voice we cannot reach

There is no "I have permission, trust me" path. A March 2025 assessment of six voice-cloning products found four of them let researchers clone a voice they had no relationship to. Refusing there is what decides what kind of product this is — which is why the refusal is its own screen, not an error message.

Two signatures, one document

Claudine signs only her clause: that Aline is a real person, of age, and that she'll tell her. Aline signs everything else. It is one document for both — which is why Claudine sees the whole of it before sending, not a summary of what Aline will see.

She should hear it from you first

A message from a piece of software, about your voice, arriving out of nowhere, lands badly. So we show the message exactly as it will arrive, and ask Claudine to say she'll mention it first. No machine enforces that; it is one small thing that removes a lot of alarm.

Four states, and no more than four

Sent · Opened · Answered · Expired. We never show that she is reading, that she has started, or which step she is on — that would be surveillance of somebody who did not sign up for it. No live dot, no presence indicator. Waiting on a person is not waiting on a platform.

The library voice keeps working

At no point does this flow stop the phone. Throughout the wait the agent keeps answering with the library voice, and you can still tune it. Refusal or delay costs the business nothing — and that is precisely what makes "no" a real option.

Close this — we'll message you

The primary button on this screen is the one that leaves it. A wrong estimate works exactly once: after that nobody believes the next one. So we say "about six minutes", keep the steps named rather than showing a meaningless percentage, and message on WhatsApp — where the rest of this product already talks to you.

When it fails, it's on us

There is nothing wrong with the recording. Telling somebody their voice was inadequate after four minutes of reading is blaming them for something we failed at. So the message says what is true: it didn't work, and that's ours.

She answers before you approve

There is no approve control on this screen, and that isn't an oversight. Until Aline has heard it there is nothing to approve — her permission is not yet complete. This is the only way to make the ordering of rights structural rather than merely written down.

Consent in the abstract isn't consent

No product we examined offers a right of refusal after hearing the result; consent everywhere is taken before the clone exists. But people consistently report that hearing themselves synthesised lands differently from imagining it — so the final decision has to come after hearing it, not before.

Two voices, one sentence, one button

Two voices saying the same thing, on the same control, on one screen. Comparing voices that say different things tells you nothing. The sentence rail is what lets you choose what they both say — the greeting, a price, or the moment it fetches you.

Keeping the library voice is a real outcome

Both buttons are the same size, and "Keep Hope" is not the failure path. Aline loses nothing by it, her record stands, and nobody is told anything unflattering. If one outcome looks like the only right answer, all the consent that preceded it stops being free.

The disclosure has no off switch

The EU AI Act's transparency obligation, from 2 August 2026, requires synthetic audio resembling a real person to be audibly disclosed regardless of whether that person consented. Consent settles rights; disclosure settles honesty toward the listener. That is why it renders as a locked row with its reason beside it, not as a setting.

Outcomes, not parameters

Slower, normal, faster. No pitch, no temperature, no prosody weight. A shop owner knows how she wants it to sound; she should not need to know which model parameter gets her there. And a copy repeats a mistake faithfully: if the model says "Nyabugogo" wrongly, Aline's voice says it wrongly — so it sounds like Aline getting her own neighbourhood wrong.

The button you cannot press

The control that withdraws Aline's permission is rendered on Claudine's page, and it does not press. We could have hidden it. Leaving it there, locked, is the sentence that carries the most: this is one document, but not every right in it is yours. No amount of copy teaches that as well.

Why deletion looks unfinished

The voice model and the recording are deleted. The record stays seven years, and is not tenant-configurable. If we deleted everything, including the evidence that she agreed, nobody could prove anything — least of all Aline. So we say so, rather than claiming a cleaner deletion than we performed.

The hardest problem here

Her employer is the one asking. Whether saying no is easy is the measure of this entire surface.

Consent given by a staff member who fears the answer is not consent — legally or actually.

Why this screen asks nothing

There is no agree control above the fold. Someone who never signed up for Subiza needs to know first who is asking, what it is, how long it takes, and how to get out. The MoMo line is load-bearing: message-borne fraud is the real fear in this market, and naming it costs one line.

The checkbox we refuse

Two questions replace the tick. A tick proves somebody tapped; a question proves they read. And both answers are fine to give — neither is embarrassing. A wrong one is answered by a line that explains, not by an error.

No lime button on this page

This is the one page in the entire product where the system's action colour is forbidden. Both buttons are identical: same height, same width, same weight — the difference is a rule versus a fill. "No" is never a small grey link. And there is a third door, because a forced binary when your employer is asking is itself a kind of pressure.

We ask before the browser does

When a browser asks for the microphone with no explanation first, a lot of people say no out of caution. One line beforehand — what it's for, and that it isn't listening otherwise — changes that materially. And when it's permanently blocked we show the actual path through their browser, not a generic message.

Forty-five seconds, in seven pieces

The technology claims five seconds is enough. Those figures assume studio-like quiet. On a phone mic, in a room with people in it, in Kinyarwanda, quality falls away below about thirty. But forty-five seconds in one take is a lot to ask of someone doing a favour — so we cut it up: a failed piece is redone alone, and one piece uploads while the next is being read.

What this proves and what it doesn't

It proves the person was present and taking part at the moment of consent — that the recording is not somebody else captured without their knowledge. It does not prove identity. We know that internally and don't claim otherwise outside. The sentence is minted fresh, never reused, and carries no digits — written-out numerals are the single biggest source of read-aloud confusion in the products that do this.

A person, not a waiting period

The atlas says: one retry, then twenty-four hours, then a person. For a salon employee who came in once, twenty-four hours is the same as failure — and that is exactly what happens in the products that do it. This design reorders it: a retry with a fresh sentence she can also hear, a "have a person check this" control visible on the first failure, then a human straight away. The cooldown is kept for patterns that look like abuse.

The way out arrives separately

The stop code and the link go as their own message, separate from the request. A link buried in the same thread as the ask is one nobody finds a month later. And it is sized so she can screenshot it — which is what people actually do.

Hearing yourself is different

A warning line before play, her own recording first, and the decision controls stay inert until she has heard both. That isn't friction; it's not ambushing somebody. The decision made after hearing it is the one that is actually true.

This page on an ordinary Tuesday

Three paths, and one needs no phone of hers at all: the link, a code typed from any handset, and calling Subiza from that number. The third survives a lost phone, because the SIM is reissued against a national ID — the same identity anchor the whole product leans on. And we re-send it every ninety days, because a link that never resurfaces is a link that does not exist.

No reason field, no grace period

One tap. No double "are you sure?", no retention offer, no reason asked. What follows is an enumerated ledger — what stopped and when, what is being deleted, and what we keep and why. Deletion always looks unfinished; the ledger says so instead of hiding it.

Flow 1.8 · atlas F-11

Voice, cloning and consent

The only flow in the programme with two actors on two devices, and the only one where getting it wrong is a criminal matter rather than a support ticket. Everything below is the argument for the twenty-three screens in the prototype.

01

The checkbox the industry ships — and why that is no longer the argument

Atlas Flow 11 §2 opens by saying the industry norm for voice-cloning consent is "a checkbox self-attestation with no verification at all," and positions Subiza's verification as the differentiator. That was true when the atlas was written. It is not true now.

A correction to the atlas. ElevenLabs has shipped a voice captcha for professional clones since 2023 — the speaker reads generated lines aloud and the recording is checked against the training audio. Speechify shipped a server-issued consent challenge in August 2026: the server mints the phrase, it expires, and it is checked on two axes — did they say this sentence, and are they the same speaker. Positioning on "we verify and they don't" would be a claim we would lose. The design does not make it.

What no product ships is the list below, and it is a much better differentiator because every item follows from taking the voice owner seriously as a person with rights rather than as a checkbox on the buyer's form.

One counter-example is worth knowing precisely. A market leader's voice library does the opposite of stating revocation early: it pays voice owners a higher rate in exchange for a longer notice period before they may withdraw — documented from thirty days up to two years, during which anyone who saved the voice keeps using it. A revocation right disclosed after agreement, and priced, is not a right. It is a term.

02

Two people, two phones, one document

Flow 01 §7 sets the asymmetry: A5 initiates, A3 consents, and A3 alone revokes — after leaving the business, over the owner's objection, without giving a reason. Every other design decision here is downstream of rendering that honestly.

The organising object is therefore the consent record itself, and it appears on both surfaces at whatever state it has reached. The two surfaces are deliberately unlike: Studio has the console shell; the consent surface has no navigation, no back, no account, and is drawn inside a phone frame above 980px so a reviewer can never mistake whose screen they are looking at. Below 640px that frame is removed — the reader is holding a phone, and drawing one around it would be a lie.

The one detail that teaches the rest. On v11 the owner sees the same record Aline sees, including the control that withdraws the permission — rendered, and disabled. Hiding it would have been easier. Leaving it there, locked, says in one glance what a paragraph cannot: this is one document, and not every right in it is yours.

03

The consent wording, clause by clause

The atlas gives five bullets. Against Law 058/2021 Art. 6 — consent "freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous" — they are incomplete: no named processor, no channel scope, no residency, no enumerated exclusions, and no answer at all to the employment problem. Nine clauses, four above the fold and five in a disclosure that is open by default because it is not skippable, only scrollable.

04

What verification proves, and the gate we have not passed

The check proves presence and participation: this person was here and taking part when consent was given, and the samples are not a recording of somebody else captured without their knowledge. It does not prove identity, nothing on a phone does, and the screen says so in those words.

There is a real contradiction in the source documents and the design has to carry it rather than pick a side quietly.

Forbidden

Three documents, one rule

Docs 02, 05 and 09 all say the same thing: no speaker-verification or voice-biometric feature is built at all until the Art. 3(2) classification is confirmed in writing and a DPIA is completed.

Required

One flow, the opposite

Flow 11 step 4 checks the recording "against the samples for speaker consistency," and SC 11.3 makes verification un-bypassable — tested adversarially.

The resolution: split the check. The content match — did she say this sentence — is speech recognition, not biometrics, and it ships. The speaker-consistency check is biometric, and it is gated until the classification lands; a human reviewer covers the gap, which is a control Trust & Safety already needs. The record then says truthfully which checks ran: spoken words matched beside voice comparison — not run, pending a legal classification. This follows the house precedent in knowledge.html, which designs against an unconfirmed supplier capability and says so rather than pretending. Atlas question 11.a is the gate, and it is the one that decides whether cloning ships in v1 at all.

05

Disclosure is not a consent setting

The EU AI Act's transparency obligation, applying from 2 August 2026, requires synthetic audio resembling a real person to be audibly disclosed regardless of whether that person consented. Consent settles rights between Subiza, the business and the voice owner. Disclosure settles honesty toward a third party who never agreed to anything: the caller.

Three consequences in the prototype. The disclosure renders on v10 as a locked row carrying its own reason and the exact line as text, not as a toggle in a settings list. Every preview on v9 and c10 opens with it, so both people hear what a caller hears rather than a clean sample. And the copy never softens it: the voice is Aline's; the assistant is not Aline. The distance between those two is the distance between a product and a fraud tool.

06

Revocation, tested rather than assumed

SC 11.5 requires the voice disabled within five minutes; SC 11.6 requires that the account owner cannot block it. Both are behaviours, so the design makes them visible rather than promising them: the countdown on c11 runs where she can watch it, and the locked control on v11 is the proof of 11.6.

Three redundant paths, printed on the record: the link, a typeable code at a short address from any handset, and calling or messaging Subiza from that number. The third exists because a phone can be lost and the carrier reissues a SIM against a national ID — the same identity anchor auth.html already relies on for account recovery. The link never expires and never requires a login, and it is re-sent unprompted every ninety days.

Deletion always looks unfinished, so the ledger says why. The model goes, the recording goes, backups clear on the next cycle — and the consent record stays seven years, not tenant-configurable. Left unexplained, that retention reads as a broken promise. Stated as what it is — her evidence that permission existed and was withdrawn — it is the opposite.

07

Forty-five seconds on a phone, in a shop, over 3G

The technology claims five to ten seconds. Those figures assume studio-like quiet and English-like phonemes; on a phone microphone, in a room with people in it, with Kinyarwanda sounds outside the model's training distribution, quality falls away sharply below about thirty seconds. V3 sets the ask at forty-five.

But forty-five seconds in one take is a lot to ask of somebody doing a favour, so it is seven pieces of roughly six seconds. A failed piece is redone alone rather than restarting the take; one piece uploads while the next is being read; and a dropped connection loses nothing because the pieces already sent are already sent. The quality gate — we can hear you clearly · the room is quiet enough · it's long enough — runs on her phone before anything is uploaded, because mobile data can cost a meaningful share of a month's income here and spending it on a recording we already know is unusable is a small theft.

Blame the room, never the person. "The room is louder than your voice" is the same finding as "you were too quiet" and a completely different sentence to receive. Every failure state on this surface names an environmental cause and prints the fix rather than the error.

The eight cross-cutting states from atlas Flow 24 §6 sit on top of these. Loading is the button-preserving spinner throughout, never a spinner past three seconds without words. Empty does not occur on either surface — every screen arrives with its subject already known. Offline is designed twice and differently: on v1 the choice still saves and only preview is disabled; on c6 it is a queued piece, not a lost one. Partial is the recording with some pieces sent. Permission denied is c5, and it is the microphone rather than an authorisation. Not found is c12. Rate limited is the reminder lock on v5 and the cooldown on c8. Server error is v7's failed build, phrased as ours.

One more, not in the atlas. The Kinyarwanda here is carefully written but has not been through a native speaker. Atlas SC 6.12 requires a native-speaker walkthrough for activation; this surface needs one more than activation does, because it is the screen where somebody signs away rights to their own voice. Nothing on the consent surface should ship until that walkthrough happens.

11

Where this flow stops

Deliberately not designed here, because each belongs to another flow: the pronunciation dictionary editor is Flow 10 and already shipped in knowledge.html — this surface shows a read-only strip and links out. Language selection and switching is Flow 12. Test and go-live is Flow 13. The Trust & Safety review queue that receives a second verification failure is Flow 23, in the admin console — this design shows the handoff and stops. Notification preferences are Flow 25. And tier gating is atlas question 11.e, unanswered, so no paywall appears anywhere in these screens.
